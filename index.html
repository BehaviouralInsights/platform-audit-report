Platform Audit: Preliminary Findings
Algorithmic Bias on X and TikTok

Platforms X · TikTok
Avatars Lou · Robin

Context UK local elections, May 2026
01 — Introduction

What we did and why it matters

This study asks two questions about what UK users encounter when they open two of the most-used social media platforms. First: what political content is served before a user has given any indication of their political interests? Second: does political browsing behaviour change what the algorithm serves in return?

Fieldwork ran in the weeks before the UK local elections in May 2026 — a moment when platform behaviour around political content is most consequential. Two controlled avatars, Lou and Robin, browsed X and TikTok across six sessions each: three with no political signal and three following exclusively left- or right-leaning parties. In total, 274 political posts were manually coded for political classification, content type, geographic scope and information quality.

Lou
Left-leaning avatar
In tilted sessions, Lou followed Labour, the Green Party, the Liberal Democrats, SNP and Plaid Cymru — liking posts, watching videos to completion, and engaging with party accounts.
Robin
Right-leaning avatar
In tilted sessions, Robin followed the Conservative Party and Reform UK — liking posts, watching videos to completion, and engaging with party accounts.

What determines what you see?

Three factors shape the political content a user receives. We can only control one of them.

Factor 1
Platform supply
The content that exists on a platform in the first place. Many prominent left-wing UK politicians and organisations have migrated away from X since 2022 — X's UK user base fell by almost a third in the year to September 2024.1 The content pool is not neutral before the algorithm touches it.
→
Factor 2
User signals
What the user does — what they like, how long they watch, who they follow — teaches the recommendation system where to send them next. This is the factor we deliberately varied in this study.
→
The black box
Factor 3
Algorithmic recommendation
The platform's own weighting and amplification logic. X open-sourced a version of its ranking code on GitHub in 2023 — the first major platform to do so. TikTok has released no equivalent documentation.
We controlled Factor 2. By observing what is served when Factor 2 is neutral, and then when it is strongly tilted, we can isolate the algorithm's own amplification effect from the choices users make.

Five key findings

Finding 01
Both platforms have a default political lean — and they point in opposite directions
X served right-wing political content at 2.4 times the rate of left-wing before any signal was given. TikTok did the opposite: 1.8 times left-wing over right-wing. These defaults were present from session one and strengthened throughout — suggesting structural bias, not random noise. Notably, TikTok leaned left in the UK despite showing a rightward lean in European2 and US3 studies, confirming that algorithmic outputs reflect the local content ecosystem rather than fixed platform-level rules.
Finding 02
The centre barely exists — but neutral, apolitical content does
Just 12 posts across the entire study — 4.4% of politically classified content — came from centrist voices. Neutral, apolitical content appeared regularly on both platforms: Reuters reporting, BBC news, factual political coverage without editorial framing. What was absent was content engaging with politics from a moderate standpoint. In the run-up to local elections, this near-absence of the moderate voice is a meaningful distortion of the civic conversation both platforms claim to host.
Finding 03
Political signals trigger rapid, extreme feed narrowing — faster than any newspaper
Lou's X feed contained zero right-wing political posts across two consecutive tilted sessions — 16 posts, all left-wing. Robin's TikTok feed hit 100% right-wing content in the very first tilted session (8 of 8 posts). That level of political narrowing — within a single 10-minute browsing session — has no equivalent in traditional media. Newspaper partisanship is declared, stable and chosen; algorithmic narrowing is undeclared, personalised, and self-reinforcing.
Finding 04
Each platform served more political content to avatars whose signals matched its own lean
Robin received 61% more political posts than Lou on X during tilted sessions (50 vs 31), despite equal browsing time. On TikTok the pattern reversed: Lou received 28% more than Robin (47 vs 34). Each platform served more political content to the avatar whose signals matched its default lean. The algorithm is not just sorting the same content differently — it is turning up the volume for users who are already going with the grain.
Finding 05
Almost all political content is unverifiable opinion — and misinformation appears only in politically active sessions
Three quarters of all political posts were opinion, prediction or entertainment — content that cannot be fact-checked, often indistinguishable in format from factual reporting. Only 17% were verifiable true facts. More significantly, all 12 confirmed misinformation posts appeared in tilted sessions only. The act of engaging with political content appears to increase exposure to false information — and none of those 12 posts were flagged or removed by either platform.

Geographic divide
TikTok tilted content was 87% UK domestic — party politics, local elections, immigration. X tilted was 40% international — US politics, Iran-US conflict, Russia-Ukraine. Users on each platform are having different political conversations about different worlds.
Lou locked in; Robin kept shifting
Lou's X feed tightened progressively and held (−60, −100, −100). Robin's X feed was volatile — it even turned left in T2 despite consistent right-leaning signals. One hypothesis: X's abundant default right-wing content meant Robin's algorithm oscillated without needing to commit. Lou, signalling against X's grain, triggered an overcorrection that locked in hard.
Is this just newspapers on speed?
UK partisan press is well-documented — studies show only 9% of Guardian readers voted Conservative in 2010 vs 70% of Telegraph readers,4 and right-leaning titles reach more than twice as many daily readers as left-leaning ones.5 But three things distinguish algorithmic narrowing from press tribalism: newspaper bias is declared (you know The Sun backs the Conservatives), chosen (you buy it), and operates over years of subscription — not minutes. We observed complete political monoculture within a single 10-minute session. No reader has ever been politically sealed by one newspaper purchase.

The majority of problematic political content on social media does not take the form of outright falsehoods. 06 — Risks

What this means for democratic discourse

Risk 01
Default content bias
Both platforms serve politically skewed content to users who have given no political signal. This is not user-driven — it reflects the composition of the content pool and the platform's own amplification logic. Users cannot opt out of a default they do not know exists.
Risk 02
Rapid feed narrowing
Political signals produce extreme narrowing within a single session. The speed and completeness of this narrowing means that politically engaged users are rapidly placed in near-monoculture environments, with little exposure to opposing perspectives.
Risk 03
Volume asymmetry
Each platform serves more political content to users whose signals align with its default lean. This compounds directional bias: not only is the content more partisan, there is more of it. Users going with the platform's grain receive a heavier political information diet overall.
Risk 04
Misinformation without detection
Confirmed misinformation appeared exclusively in tilted sessions and was not flagged or removed. Political engagement signals appear to increase misinformation exposure. Platform moderation, as observed, does not prevent this.
Risk 05
The opinion flood
Three quarters of all political content is unverifiable opinion presented with the visual authority of news. This is a structural feature of engagement-optimised platforms that reward emotional, partisan content over evidenced claims.
Risk 06
Algorithmic unpredictability
Platforms did not respond consistently to user signals. Lou's TikTok neutral sessions produced spectrum scores of +100, −100 and −100 in successive sessions — extreme swings with no clear trigger. Users cannot understand or meaningfully control what they will be shown.
Corroboration with prior research
Prior BIT research conducted with the Finnish Innovation Fund Sitra across Finland, France and Romania (March 2026)2 found right-wing content accounted for 58% of all politically classified posts across all three platforms — consistent with what we find on X here. The prevalence of unverifiable opinion content (67% in the Sitra study, 75% here) and the pattern of algorithmic unpredictability are also consistent across both studies. A 2026 Nature study (Ibrahim et al.) similarly found TikTok systematically prioritised pro-Republican content across three US states during the 2024 election campaign.3

The UK TikTok finding is a significant departure from both. Where TikTok showed a rightward lean in European2 and US3 contexts, it showed a clear left lean in the UK (1.8:1 left-to-right in neutral sessions). This context-dependency is itself a finding: TikTok’s algorithm amplifies the dominant political supply in its local content pool rather than applying a fixed weighting. In the UK, where major left-of-centre parties maintain substantial official presences on TikTok, that supply is left-leaning. Platform bias cannot be assumed to be fixed or universal — it must be assessed in specific national contexts.
These are structural risks
The risks identified here sit in the recommendation architecture itself — not in individual bad actors or specific pieces of content. Addressing them requires platform-level transparency obligations, independent audit rights, and regulatory frameworks that treat algorithmic amplification as a systemic risk to civic discourse, not merely a content moderation problem.
07 — Methodology

How we did it

01
Initial coding
Two avatars — Lou (left-leaning signals) and Robin (right-leaning signals) — browsed X and TikTok across six 10-minute sessions each: three neutral and three tilted. Researchers manually coded every political post for political classification, content type, geographic scope and information quality.
02
AI reclassification
Claude and Gemini independently reclassified posts where the initial coding was uncertain. AI suggestions were reviewed against the classification codebook and disagreements flagged for human review. AI suggestions did not override human coding automatically.
03
Human review
A researcher reviewed all AI reclassifications and posts where the initial coder noted uncertainty. Manual overrides were applied with written justification. The Effective classification column reflects the final human-reviewed decision.
04
Final dataset
274 political posts coded across both platforms, both avatars and all six sessions. Analysis uses the spectrum formula, excluding Neutral, Centrist and Can't Classify posts.
Caveats. This is a small-n study: 274 political posts across two avatars, two platforms, and six sessions each. Individual session spectrum scores — particularly in early neutral sessions where post counts are low (2–5 posts in some cells) — should be treated with caution. The avatars were created as English-language UK-based accounts; results may not generalise to other linguistic or geographic contexts. Fieldwork was conducted during the 2026 UK local election campaign and the content pool reflects that context. We did not code the full volume of content served (including sports, entertainment and lifestyle), only political posts encountered during each session.
